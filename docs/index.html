Behind the tool

  • Adoption follows one S-curve for end demand. Each layer follows that curve shifted by its timing offset and scaled by its steepness, so upstream revenue is derived from the same demand rather than an independent forecast. Layer revenue is the value pool times layer adoption times the layer's share, with the share drifting each year at the rate set for that phase (negative drift stands for commoditisation).
  • Build capex is spread evenly over the build years, starting in the year you set (default year 0). After that, sustaining spend equals build capex divided by asset life. Split the life of the technology from the life of the asset when you set it.
  • Capex model. The default, sustaining spend (v0.1), spends build capex ÷ asset life every year after the build. Vintage cohorts (opt-in) treats each year’s build spend as a cohort replaced at the end of its life (rounded to whole years), at the original spend × (1 − unit-cost decline)life; replacement after year 15 is not charged, and value beyond year 15 uses a normalised sustaining spend, capex × (1 − decline)15 ÷ life. Replacement is assumed to happen on time, so capacity stays at 100% after the initial build. Unit-cost decline lowers the owner’s replacement cost, and competition passes a share of it (pass-through, 0 to 1, default 0.5 as a placeholder with no view behind it) to customers as lower prices. That erosion runs through share drift: each year’s share multiplier is (1 + phase drift) × (1 − pass-through × decline). Drift covers other commoditisation; pass-through covers erosion caused by cheaper capacity. Value beyond year 15 uses the year-15 share after pass-through. Stranded value is a diagnostic, not a cash item. Caveat: pass-through lowers a layer’s revenue without raising demand. If demand is price-elastic, cheaper capacity could raise revenue instead; price elasticity is not modelled. The entry price under the premium definition covers the initial build only (replacement before the entry year is not priced), and debt covers the initial build only. Even at zero decline, vintage cohorts differ from sustaining spend: replacement arrives in lumps at end of life rather than as a steady charge from the end of the build.
  • Capacity limit (an assumption, not data): a layer can only earn on capacity it has built. Capacity is taken to scale linearly with build spend, so revenue uses the lower of layer adoption and the share of build capex spent so far. Without this, starting the build later would always look better, because spend would get cheaper while revenue stayed the same. It is a first-order stand-in for capacity utilisation, to be refined by vintage capex. At the default settings it never binds.
  • Value beyond year 15. By default (the v0.1 model) it is your multiple times year-15 cash after sustaining spend, floored at zero. The opt-in growing perpetuity instead treats year-15 cash as growing for ever at a long-run rate g, discounted at your rate r: a multiple of (1 + g) ÷ (r − g). Growth is a placeholder input (default 0, unsourced) and must stay at least 1 point below the discount rate. At g = 0 the perpetuity is 1 ÷ r, 10x at 10%, twice the default multiple of 5, so switching mode at default settings raises every layer’s value. Asset life runs to 100 years and the multiple to 30x, so long-lived assets can be represented; with a 15-year horizon a longer life changes only sustaining spend, the life flag and the terminal value.
  • Present value uses your discount rate and is stated at your entry year. With the premium definition you pay the build cost already sunk, marked up, plus later build at the same mark-up. With the multiple definition you pay a multiple of next-year operating cash, plus later build at cost. Break-even solves for the price at which present value is zero.
  • Not yet modelled: margins and drift that respond to how full the built capacity is. The capacity limit above only caps revenue at what has been built; it does not change prices. Deferred until the sensitivity ranking shows it matters.
  • This is a straight-line model. There is no competition between layers, no price war feedback, and no check that shares add up beyond the warning above.
  • Demand evidence is your judgement on a 1 to 5 scale. The model does not verify it.
  • One discount rate for every layer, and no probabilities: each scenario is a single path, not a distribution.
  • Past episodes: the case library can hold historical cases filled in as of a date with cited sources, but none is published yet. Until there are scored cases, treat outputs as a way to see which assumptions carry the result, not as calibrated odds. Even then, a handful of cases scored by people who know the outcome is a sanity check, not calibration.
